This paper evaluates whether a stacked long short-term memory (LSTM) forecast of the traffic speed ahead makes an ecological predictive cruise controller for an electric vehicle (EV) more energy-efficient. A 2,500 kg EV model with a single-speed reduction gear, motor torque and power limits, and blended regenerative and friction braking follows the EPA UDDS (city), HWFET (highway) and US06 (aggressive) drive cycles, none of which is used for training or tuning.

The LSTM (two stacked layers of 128 and 64 units with batch normalization and dropout p = 0.2) forecasts the reference speed 2 s ahead from its last 10 s. It is trained on 240 synthetic traffic episodes in three driving styles, split by episode into training and validation, with early stopping on validation. Training is deterministic for a given seed and is repeated with five seeds.

Four controllers are compared: bang-bang, PID, PID with deceleration-only feedforward (LSTM-PID), and a receding-horizon model predictive controller (LSTM-MPC) solved with L-BFGS-B. Each forecast-based controller is run with four forecasts: persistence, constant-acceleration extrapolation, the LSTM, and an oracle that knows the true future. Because energy can always be saved by tracking the reference more loosely, energy is compared at equal tracking error by sweeping the MPC energy weight.

With a perfect 2 s preview, the MPC matches the PID's tracking with 2.6%, 0.7% and 4.3% less energy on the city, highway and aggressive cycles. The LSTM recovers little of this: over five training seeds the saving is 1.0% ± 0.8, 0.0% ± 0.6 and −0.9% ± 1.7. Its forecasts are about as accurate as constant-acceleration extrapolation, and their step-to-step variation raises the MPC's jerk 2.5–2.8× above the oracle MPC's. The deceleration feedforward does not change PID energy (≤ 0.4%).
